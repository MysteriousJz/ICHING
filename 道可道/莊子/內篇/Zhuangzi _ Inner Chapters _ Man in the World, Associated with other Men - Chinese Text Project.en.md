# Man in the World, Associated with other Men (人間世)
Source: Zhuangzi _ Inner Chapters _ Man in the World, Associated with other Men - Chinese Text Project.html
Status: complete (9 sections)

## Section 1

**ZH:** 顏回見仲尼請行。曰：「奚之？」曰：「將之衛。」曰：「奚為焉？」曰：「回聞衛君，其年壯，其行獨，輕用其國，而不見其過，輕用民死，死者以國量乎澤，若蕉，民其无如矣。回嘗聞之夫子曰：『治國去之，亂國就之，醫門多疾。』願以所聞思其則，庶幾其國有瘳乎！」仲尼曰：「譆！若殆往而刑耳！夫道不欲雜，雜則多，多則擾，擾則憂，憂而不救。古之至人，先存諸己，而後存諸人。所存於己者未定，何暇至於暴人之所行！且若亦知夫德之所蕩，而知之所為出乎哉？德蕩乎名，知出乎爭。名也者，相軋也；知也者，爭之器也。二者凶器，非所以盡行也。且德厚信矼，未達人氣；名聞不爭，未達人心。而彊以仁義繩墨之言術暴人之前者，是以人惡有其美也，命之曰菑人。菑人者，人必反菑之，若殆為人菑夫！且苟為悅賢而惡不肖，惡用而求有以異？若唯无詔，王公必將乘人而鬭其捷。而目將熒之，而色將平之，口將營之，容將形之，心且成之。是以火救火，以水救水，名之曰益多，順始无窮。若殆以不信厚言，必死於暴人之前矣。且昔者桀殺關龍逢，紂殺王子比干，是皆脩其身以下傴拊人之民，以下拂其上者也，故其君因其脩以擠之。是好名者也。昔者堯攻叢枝、胥敖，禹攻有扈，國為虛厲，身為刑戮，其用兵不止，其求實无已。是皆求名、實者也，而獨不聞之乎？名、實者，聖人之所不能勝也，而況若乎！雖然，若必有以也，嘗以語我來！」顏回曰：「端而虛，勉而一，則可乎？」曰：「惡！惡可？夫以陽為充孔揚，采色不定，常人之所不違，因案人之所感，以求容與其心。名之曰日漸之德不成，而況大德乎！將執而不化，外合而內不訾，其庸詎可乎！」「然則我內直而外曲，成而上比。內直者，與天為徒。與天為徒者，知天子之與己皆天之所子，而獨以己言蘄乎而人善之，蘄乎而人不善之邪？若然者，人謂之童子，是之謂與天為徒。外曲者，與人之為徒也。擎、跽、曲拳，人臣之禮也，人皆為之，吾敢不為邪！為人之所為者，人亦无疵焉，是之謂與人為徒。成而上比者，與古為徒。其言雖教，讁之實也。古之有也，非吾有也。若然者，雖直不為病，是之謂與古為徒。若是，則可乎？」仲尼曰：「惡！惡可？大多政，法而不諜，雖固，亦无罪。雖然，止是耳矣，夫胡可以及化！猶師心者也。」

**EN:** Yan Hui (顏回) came to see Confucius and asked leave to depart. "Where are you going?" "To Wei (衛)." "What will you do there?" He said: "I have heard that the lord of Wei is in the prime of life and his conduct is self-willed. He uses his state recklessly and does not see his own faults; he spends his people's lives recklessly, and the dead fill the state like weeds measuring out a marsh, until the people have nowhere left to turn. [uncertain: 死者以國量乎澤，若蕉 — a tangled clause; read here as "the dead fill the state like plantains blanketing a marsh."] I once heard you say, Master: 'Leave the well-ordered state; go to the disordered one — at the physician's gate are many of the sick.' I wish to think out a rule from what I have heard; perhaps this state may yet be healed!" Confucius said: "Ah! You will most likely just go and get yourself executed. The Way does not want admixture; admixture leads to multiplicity; multiplicity to confusion; confusion to worry — worry with no way out. The utmost men of old first planted it in themselves, and only then in others. While what is planted in yourself is not yet settled, what leisure have you to meddle in the conduct of a violent man! And do you know wherein virtue is dissipated, and what knowledge comes out of? Virtue is dissipated in fame; knowledge comes out of contention. Fame is a thing of mutual crushing; knowledge is a weapon of strife. Both are instruments of ill omen — not means by which to walk one's course to the end. Moreover, though your virtue is thick and your good faith solid, you have not yet penetrated that man's temperament; though your name is free of contention, you have not yet penetrated that man's heart. [uncertain: 信矼 — 矼 read with the commentators as "solid, dependable."] To insist on parading words of humaneness, rightness, measure and rule before a violent man is to make your own excellence shine by another's disgrace; this is called being a blight upon others. One who blights others will surely be blighted in return — you are likely to be blighted by that man! And if indeed he delights in the worthy and hates the unworthy, what need has he of you to make himself different? Unless you utter nothing at all, the lord and his officers will seize upon you and contend for the upper hand. Then your eyes will grow dazzled, your expression will fall, your mouth will babble excuses, your countenance will conform, and your heart will go along and complete it. That is to fight fire with fire, to fight flood with flood — it is called adding to the excess. Go along with the beginning, and it never ends. Press sincere words on one who does not trust you, and you will surely die before this violent man. Of old, Jie (桀) killed Guan Longfeng (關龍逢) and Zhou (紂) killed Prince Bigan (比干). Both had cultivated themselves until, stooping down, they cherished the people's people, and as subjects they offended those above; therefore their lords seized upon their very cultivation to crush them. These were lovers of fame. Of old, Yao attacked Congzhi (叢枝) and Xu'ao, and Yu attacked Youhu (有扈); their states were turned into empty wastes and their persons into executed corpses. Their use of arms never ceased, their seeking after substance never ended. These were all seekers of fame and substance — have you alone not heard of them? Fame and substance even the sage could not overcome — how much less you! Still, you must have something in mind. Come — tell me of it." Yan Hui said: "Upright and empty, diligent and single-minded — would that do?" Confucius said: "No! How could that do? That man fills out his overbearingness with a strutting display; his moods are unsettled, ordinary men dare not cross him, for he suppresses what others feel in order to indulge his own heart. One may call it a virtue that does not grow even day by day — how much less a great virtue! He will hold fast and refuse to be transformed; outwardly he will agree with you while inwardly he never finds fault with himself. How could that possibly do!" "Then let me be inwardly straight and outwardly bending, complete in myself and comparing upward with the ancients. To be inwardly straight is to be a companion of Heaven. A companion of Heaven knows that the Son of Heaven and himself are alike children of Heaven; why then should he long for men to approve his words, or long that they not disapprove them? Such a one men call a child — this is called being a companion of Heaven. To be outwardly bending is to be a companion of men. Bearing the tablet, kneeling, bowing with folded hands — these are the rites of a subject. All men perform them; how dare I not? Doing what others do, others find no fault in one — this is called being a companion of men. To be complete within and compare upward is to be a companion of the ancients. Though one's words instruct, they carry the substance of rebuke — yet they belonged to the ancients, not to me. In this way, though one is blunt, one gives no offense — this is called being a companion of the ancients. Would that do?" Confucius said: "No! How could that do? Too many governing devices, patterned but untested — though sound, they will at least bring you no blame. But that is as far as it goes; how could they ever bring about transformation! You are still making the mind your teacher."

## Section 2

**ZH:** 顏回曰：「吾无以進矣，敢問其方。」仲尼曰：「齋，吾將語若！有而為之，其易邪？易之者，皞天不宜。」顏回曰：「回之家貧，唯不飲酒、不茹葷者數月矣。若此，則可以為齋乎？」曰：「是祭祀之齋，非心齋也。」回曰：「敢問心齋。」仲尼曰：「若一志，无聽之以耳而聽之以心，无聽之以心而聽之以氣。聽止於耳，心止於符。氣也者，虛而待物者也。唯道集虛。虛者，心齋也。」顏回曰：「回之未始得使，實自回也；得使之也，未始有回也。可謂虛乎？」夫子曰：「盡矣。吾語若！若能入遊其樊而无感其名，入則鳴，不入則止。无門无毒，一宅而寓於不得已，則幾矣。絕迹易，无行地難。為人使，易以偽；為天使，難以偽。聞以有翼飛者矣，未聞以无翼飛者也；聞以有知知者矣，未聞以无知知者也。瞻彼闋者，虛室生白，吉祥止止。夫且不止，是之謂坐馳。夫徇耳目內通而外於心知，鬼神將來舍，而況人乎！是萬物之化也，禹、舜之所紐也，伏戲、几蘧之所行終，而況散焉者乎！」

**EN:** Yan Hui said: "I can advance no further. May I ask the method?" Confucius said: "Fast, and I will tell you. To do it with a something in mind — is that easy? One who finds that easy has no favor from the bright sky." Yan Hui said: "My family is poor; I have not drunk wine nor tasted strong-flavored flesh for months. Can that count as fasting?" Confucius said: "That is the fasting of sacrifice, not the fasting of the mind." Yan Hui said: "May I ask about the fasting of the mind?" Confucius said: "Make your will one. Do not listen with the ear, but listen with the mind; do not listen with the mind, but listen with the vital breath. Hearing stops at the ear; the mind stops at tallying. The vital breath is empty and waits upon things. The Way gathers in emptiness alone. Emptiness is the fasting of the mind." Yan Hui said: "Before I ever got to put it into practice, I was solidly Hui; having gotten to practice it, there has never yet begun to be a Hui. May that be called emptiness?" The Master said: "That is all. Let me tell you: if you can go in and roam within his fence without being moved by fame — when he lets you in, sing; when he does not, stop. Have no gate, show no poison; make one dwelling and lodge in the unavoidable — then you are nearly there. [uncertain: 无門无毒 — 毒 read variously as "poison" and as a loan for 竇 ("opening, outlet"); rendered "show no poison / give no purchase."] To leave no tracks is easy; to walk without touching the ground is hard. Sent on a mission by men, it is easy to be false; sent by Heaven, it is hard to be false. I have heard of flying with wings; I have not heard of flying without wings. I have heard of knowing with knowledge; I have not heard of knowing without knowledge. Gaze into that sealed emptiness: the empty room gives birth to whiteness, and good fortune halts where there is halting. If one does not halt there, that is called sitting still while racing. Let the ears and eyes commune within, and put the mind's knowing outside — then even ghosts and spirits will come to lodge with you, how much more men! This is the transformation of the ten thousand things; it was the hinge of Yu and Shun, the lifelong practice of Fuxi (伏羲) and Jiqu (几蘧) — how much more should it be for scattered, lesser men!"

## Section 3

**ZH:** 葉公子高將使於齊，問於仲尼曰：「王使諸梁也甚重，齊之待使者，蓋將甚敬而不急。匹夫猶未可動，而況諸侯乎！吾甚慄之。子常語諸梁也，曰：『凡事若小若大，寡不道以懽成。事若不成，則必有人道之患；事若成，則必有陰陽之患。若成若不成而後無患者，唯有德者能之。』吾食也，執粗而不臧，爨無欲清之人。今吾朝受命而夕飲冰，我其內熱與！吾未至乎事之情，而既有陰陽之患矣；事若不成，必有人道之患。是兩也，為人臣者不足以任之，子其有以語我來！」仲尼曰：「天下有大戒二：其一，命也；其一，義也。子之愛親，命也，不可解於心；臣之事君，義也，無適而非君也，無所逃於天地之間。是之謂大戒。是以夫事其親者，不擇地而安之，孝之至也；夫事其君者，不擇事而安之，忠之盛也；自事其心者，哀樂不易施乎前，知其不可奈何而安之若命，德之至也。為人臣子者，固有所不得已，行事之情而忘其身，何暇至於悅生而惡死！夫子其行可矣！丘請復以所聞：凡交，近則必相靡以信，遠則必忠之以言，言必或傳之。夫傳兩喜兩怒之言，天下之難者也。夫兩喜必多溢美之言，兩怒必多溢惡之言。凡溢之類妄，妄則其信之也莫，莫則傳言者殃。故法言曰：『傳其常情，無傳其溢言，則幾乎全。』且以巧鬥力者，始乎陽，常卒乎陰，大至則多奇巧；以禮飲酒者，始乎治，常卒乎亂，大至則多奇樂。凡事亦然。始乎諒，常卒乎鄙；其作始也簡，其將畢也必巨。夫言者，風波也；行者，實喪也。風波易以動，實喪易以危。故忿設無由，巧言偏辭。獸死不擇音，氣息茀然，於是並生心厲。剋核大至，則必有不肖之心應之，而不知其然也。苟為不知其然也，孰知其所終！故法言曰：『無遷令，無勸成。』過度，益也。遷令、勸成殆事，美成在久，惡成不及改，可不慎與！且夫乘物以遊心，託不得已以養中，至矣。何作為報也！莫若為致命。此其難者。」

**EN:** Zigao (子高), Duke of She (葉公), was about to go on a mission to Qi (齊), and asked Confucius: "The king has charged me, Zhuliang, with a weighty mission. Qi treats its envoys with great ceremony but no urgency. Even a common man cannot easily be moved — how much less a feudal lord! I am sorely afraid. You once said to me: 'In all affairs, small or great, few reach a happy completion except by the Way. If an affair fails, there is sure to be trouble from men; if it succeeds, there is sure to be trouble from the yin and yang. To be without trouble afterward, whether it succeeds or fails — only one of virtue can do that.' In my eating I take the coarse and do not crave the fine, and my cooks feel no need to cool themselves. Yet now I received the charge in the morning and by evening I am drinking ice — I must be burning up within! I have not yet reached the heart of the affair, and already I suffer from the yin and yang; and if it fails, I shall suffer from men as well. That is both at once, and as a subject I cannot bear it. Do tell me something!" Confucius said: "Under heaven there are two great bonds: one is destiny; one is duty. That a child loves its parents is destiny — it cannot be unbound from the heart. That a subject serves his lord is duty — wherever he goes, there is a lord; there is no escaping it between heaven and earth. These are called the great bonds. Thus one who serves his parents is at ease wherever they may be — that is the height of filial piety. One who serves his lord is at ease in whatever the task — that is the fullness of loyalty. And one who serves his own heart does not let sorrow and joy revolve before him; he knows there is nothing to be done and rests in it as destiny — that is the height of virtue. To be a subject and a child is to have what cannot be helped. Act on the reality of the matter and forget your own person — what leisure have you then to love life and hate death! You may go, sir. Let me add what I have heard. In all dealings: those near at hand must bind one another by trust; those far apart must be made faithful through words — and words must be carried by someone. To carry words between two parties who are pleased, or two who are angry, is the hardest task under heaven. When both are pleased, there must be an excess of flattering words; when both are angry, an excess of ugly words. All excess tends toward falsehood; falsehood breeds distrust; and distrust brings calamity down upon the messenger. Hence the old maxim says: 'Transmit the genuine condition; do not transmit the excessive words — then you may come through whole.' Those who contend with skill and strength begin in the light and usually end in the dark; at the extreme there is much crafty trickery. Those who drink wine according to ritual begin in order and usually end in disorder; at the extreme there is much strange indulgence. All things are like this. They begin in good faith and usually end in coarseness; their beginnings are simple, but by the end they are immense. Words are wind and waves; action is loss of substance. Wind and waves easily set things in motion; loss of substance easily brings danger. Thus wrath is stirred up for no cause, through cunning words and one-sided phrases. A dying beast does not choose its cry; its breath comes fierce, and a savage heart is born in both at once. If the reckoning is pressed too hard, an unworthy heart will answer it, without one's knowing how. And if one does not know how it happens, who knows how it will end! Hence the old maxim says: 'Do not alter the charge; do not press for completion.' To go beyond is excess. Altering the charge and pressing for completion endanger the affair. A fine completion takes long; a bad one cannot be undone in time. Can one afford not to be careful! Now ride upon things and let the heart roam; entrust yourself to the unavoidable to nourish the center — that is the utmost. Why scheme over what to report back? Nothing is better than delivering the charge as it is. That is what is difficult."

## Section 4

**ZH:** 顏闔將傅衛靈公大子，而問於蘧伯玉曰：「有人於此，其德天殺。與之為無方，則危吾國；與之為有方，則危吾身。其知適足以知人之過，而不知其所以過。若然者，吾奈之何？」蘧伯玉曰：「善哉問乎！戒之慎之，正汝身也哉！形莫若就，心莫若和。雖然，之二者有患。就不欲入，和不欲出。形就而入，且為顛為滅，為崩為蹶。心和而出，且為聲為名，為妖為孽。彼且為嬰兒，亦與之為嬰兒；彼且為無町畦，亦與之為無町畦；彼且為無崖，亦與之為無崖。達之，入於無疵。汝不知夫螳蜋乎？怒其臂以當車轍，不知其不勝任也，是其才之美者也。戒之慎之！積伐而美者以犯之，幾矣。汝不知夫養虎者乎？不敢以生物與之，為其殺之之怒也；不敢以全物與之，為其決之之怒也。時其飢飽，達其怒心。虎之與人異類而媚養己者，順也；故其殺者，逆也。夫愛馬者，以筐盛矢，以蜄盛溺。適有蚉虻僕緣，而拊之不時，則缺銜、毀首、碎胸。意有所至，而愛有所亡，可不慎邪！」

**EN:** Yan He (顏闔) was about to become tutor to the heir apparent of Duke Ling of Wei (衛靈公), and asked Qu Boyu (蘧伯玉): "There is a man here whose virtue is heaven-slain. If I keep company with him without standards, my state is endangered; if I keep company with him by standards, my own person is endangered. His knowledge is just enough to see the faults of others, but not to see whence his own faults come. What am I to do with such a one?" Qu Boyu said: "A fine question! Be warned, be careful; keep your own person upright! Outwardly, nothing is better than compliance; inwardly, nothing is better than harmony. Yet both of these carry danger. Comply without sinking in; harmonize without sticking out. If your compliance sinks in, you will be overthrown, destroyed, collapsed, and ruined. If your harmony sticks out, it will turn into sound and fame, monstrosity and calamity. If he plays the infant, play the infant with him; if he keeps no fields' borders, keep no borders with him; if he has no banks or bounds, have no bounds with him. Reach through to him, and enter into blamelessness. Do you not know the praying mantis? It rears its arms in fury to block a cartwheel, not knowing it is not up to the task — that is how dearly it prizes its own talent. Be warned, be careful! To keep parading your own excellence before him is to come near his danger. Do you not know the keeper of tigers? He dares not give it live prey, for fear of its rage in the killing; he dares not give it whole prey, for fear of its rage in the rending. He times its hunger and fullness; he reaches through to its angry heart. Tiger and man are different kinds, yet it fawns on the one who rears it — because he goes along with it. So those it kills are those who go against it. A lover of horses catches its dung in a basket and its urine in a great shell. But if a mosquito or a fly settles on it and he slaps it at the wrong moment, the horse will snap the bit, smash the headstall, and burst the chest-band. His intention went to the utmost — yet his love was undone. Can one afford not to be careful!"

## Section 5

**ZH:** 匠石之齊，至乎曲轅，見櫟社樹。其大蔽數千牛，絜之百圍，其高臨山十仞而後有枝，其可以為舟者旁十數。觀者如市，匠伯不顧，遂行不輟。弟子厭觀之，走及匠石，曰：「自吾執斧斤以隨夫子，未嘗見材如此其美也。先生不肯視，行不輟，何邪？」曰：「已矣，勿言之矣！散木也，以為舟則沈，以為棺槨則速腐，以為器則速毀，以為門戶則液樠，以為柱則蠹。是不材之木也，無所可用，故能若是之壽。」匠石歸，櫟社見夢曰：「女將惡乎比予哉？若將比予於文木邪？夫柤、梨、橘、柚、果、蓏之屬，實熟則剝，剝則辱，大枝折，小枝泄。此以其能苦其生者也，故不終其天年而中道夭，自掊擊於世俗者也。物莫不若是。且予求無所可用久矣，幾死，乃今得之，為予大用。使予也而有用，且得有此大也邪？且也，若與予也皆物也，奈何哉其相物也？而幾死之散人，又惡知散木！」匠石覺而診其夢。弟子曰：「趣取無用，則為社何邪？」曰：「密！若無言！彼亦直寄焉，以為不知己者詬厲也。不為社者，且幾有翦乎！且也，彼其所保，與眾異，以義譽之，不亦遠乎！」

**EN:** Carpenter Shi (匠石) went to Qi, and coming to Quyuan (曲轅) saw an oak at the altar of the soil. It was broad enough to shade several thousand oxen; measured with a cord, a hundred spans around; it towered over the hills for eighty feet before putting out branches, and a dozen boats could have been cut from its sides. Those who came to gaze were like a market crowd, but the master carpenter did not deign to look and walked on without pausing. His disciples feasted their eyes on it, then ran after Carpenter Shi, saying: "Since first we took up axe and adze to follow you, master, never have we seen timber as fine as this. Yet you would not even look at it, and went on without stopping. Why?" He said: "Enough! Say no more of it! It is a worthless tree. Make a boat of it and it would sink; make a coffin of it and it would rot at once; make a vessel of it and it would break at once; make a door of it and it would sweat sap; make a pillar of it and it would be worm-eaten. It is a timberless tree, of no use whatever — that is how it has lived to such a great age." When Carpenter Shi returned home, the altar oak appeared to him in a dream: "With what do you mean to compare me? Will you compare me to the fine-grained trees? The hawthorn, the pear, the orange, the pomelo, and the other fruit- and melon-bearing trees — when their fruit is ripe they are stripped; being stripped, they are abused; their great branches are broken, their small ones torn. These are trees that embitter their lives by their own gifts; therefore they do not live out their heaven-allotted years but die in mid-course, battered about by the vulgar world. All things are like this. For long I sought to be of no use whatever. I nearly died for it, but now at last I have attained it — and it is of the greatest use to me. Had I been of use, could I ever have grown this great? Besides, you and I are both mere things — what business have things sizing one another up? A worthless man at death's door — what does he know of worthless trees!" Carpenter Shi awoke and told his dream. His disciples said: "If it only aims at being useless, why does it serve as the altar tree?" He said: "Hush! Not a word! It is merely lodging there — letting itself be cursed by those who do not know it. Were it not the altar tree, would it not long since have been cut down? Besides, what it preserves is different from the crowd's; to praise or blame it by the standard of rightness — how far from the mark!"

## Section 6

**ZH:** 南伯子綦遊乎商之丘，見大木焉有異，結駟千乘，隱將芘其所藾。子綦曰：「此何木也哉？此必有異材夫！」仰而視其細枝，則拳曲而不可以為棟梁；俯而見其大根，則軸解而不可為棺槨；咶其葉，則口爛而為傷；嗅之，則使人狂酲三日而不已。子綦曰：「此果不材之木也，以至於此其大也。嗟乎！神人以此不材！」宋有荊氏者，宜楸、柏、桑。其拱把而上者，求狙猴之杙者斬之；三圍四圍，求高名之麗者斬之；七圍八圍，貴人富商之家求樿傍者斬之。故未終其天年，而中道已夭於斧斤，此材之患也。故解之以牛之白顙者，與豚之亢鼻者，與人有痔病者，不可以適河。此皆巫祝以知之矣，所以為不祥也，此乃神人之所以為大祥也。

**EN:** Nanbo Ziqi (南伯子綦), roaming upon the hill of Shang (商), saw a great and wondrous tree, beneath whose shade a thousand teams of four chariot-horses could take shelter. Ziqi said: "What manner of tree is this? Its timber must be of rare quality!" But looking up at its branches, he saw them twisted and curled, unfit for beam or ridgepole; looking down at its great trunk, he saw it split and riven, unfit for coffins. Lick its leaves, and the mouth festers and is wounded; smell it, and a man goes mad-drunk for three days on end. Ziqi said: "This is indeed a timberless tree — and that is how it has grown so great. Ah! The spirit-like man is like this timberless tree!" In Song there is a district of Jing (荊氏), suited to catalpa, cypress, and mulberry. Those of a span or two around are cut down by men seeking stakes for monkeys; those of three or four spans, by men seeking ridgepoles for lofty halls; those of seven or eight spans, by the families of nobles and rich merchants seeking single-plank coffins. So they never live out their heaven-allotted years, but fall in mid-course to axe and hatchet. This is the calamity of being timber. Thus at the exorcising sacrifice, an ox with a white forehead, a pig with an upturned snout, and a man with piles may not be offered to the river. All this the shamans and priests know well, and count these things inauspicious — and these are just what the spirit-like man counts greatly auspicious.

## Section 7

**ZH:** 支離疏者，頤隱於臍，肩高於頂，會撮指天，五管在上，兩髀為脅。挫鍼治繲，足以餬口；鼓筴播精，足以食十人。上徵武士，則支離攘臂而遊於其間；上有大役，則支離以有常疾不受功；上與病者粟，則受三鐘與十束薪。夫支離其形者，猶足以養其身，終其天年，又況支離其德者乎！」

**EN:** Splay-limb Shu (支離疏) — his chin sank into his navel, his shoulders rose above his crown, his gathered topknot pointed at the sky, the tubes of his five organs were on top, and his two thighs made his ribs. Plying the needle and taking in washing, he earned enough to fill his mouth; shaking the sieve and winnowing grain, he earned enough to feed ten men. When the ruler called up warriors, Splay-limb rolled up his sleeves and strolled about among the recruiters; when the ruler raised a great labor levy, Splay-limb, being chronically infirm, was exempted from the works; when the ruler doled out grain to the sick, he received three bushels and ten bundles of firewood. If one whose body is splayed and scattered can still nourish his person and live out his heaven-allotted years, how much more one whose virtue is splayed and scattered!

## Section 8

**ZH:** 孔子適楚，楚狂接輿遊其門曰：「鳳兮鳳兮，何如德之衰也！來世不可待，往世不可追也。天下有道，聖人成焉；天下無道，聖人生焉。方今之時，僅免刑焉。福輕乎羽，莫之知載；禍重乎地，莫之知避。已乎已乎，臨人以德！殆乎殆乎，畫地而趨！迷陽迷陽，無傷吾行！吾行卻曲，無傷吾足！」

**EN:** When Confucius went to Chu (楚), the madman Jieyu (接輿) wandered past his gate, singing: "Phoenix, oh phoenix! How your virtue has declined! The age to come cannot be waited for; the age gone by cannot be pursued. When the world has the Way, the sage is completed in it; when the world is without the Way, the sage merely lives in it. In times like these, it is enough to escape punishment. Fortune is lighter than a feather, yet none knows how to carry it; calamity is heavier than the earth, yet none knows how to avoid it. Enough, enough — confronting men with virtue! Perilous, perilous — marking out the ground to run on! Briar and thorn, briar and thorn — do not hurt my walking! I walk winding and crooked — do not hurt my feet!"

## Section 9

**ZH:** 山木自寇也，膏火自煎也。桂可食，故伐之；漆可用，故割之。人皆知有用之用，而莫知無用之用也。

**EN:** The trees of the mountain plunder themselves; the grease fries itself in the fire. Cinnamon can be eaten — therefore its tree is felled; varnish can be used — therefore its tree is cut. All men know the use of the useful, but none knows the use of the useless.
