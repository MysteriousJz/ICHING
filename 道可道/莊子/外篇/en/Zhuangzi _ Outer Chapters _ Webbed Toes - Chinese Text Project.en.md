# Webbed Toes (駢拇)
Source: Zhuangzi _ Outer Chapters _ Webbed Toes - Chinese Text Project.html
Status: complete (3 sections)

## Section 1

**ZH:** 駢拇枝指，出乎性哉！而侈於德。附贅縣疣，出乎形哉！而侈於性。多方乎仁義而用之者，列於五藏哉！而非道德之正也。是故駢於足者，連無用之肉也；枝於手者，樹無用之指也；多方駢枝於五藏之情者，淫僻於仁義之行，而多方於聰明之用也。是故駢於明者，亂五色，淫文章，青黃黼黻之煌煌非乎？而離朱是已。多於聰者，亂五聲，淫六律，金石、絲竹，黃鐘、大呂之聲非乎？而師曠是已。枝於仁者，擢德塞性以收名聲，使天下簧鼓以奉不及之法非乎？而曾、史是已。駢於辯者，纍瓦結繩竄句，遊心於堅白同異之間，而敝跬譽無用之言非乎？而楊、墨是已。故此皆多駢旁枝之道，非天下之至正也。彼正正者，不失其性命之情。故合者不為駢，而枝者不為跂；長者不為有餘，短者不為不足。是故鳧脛雖短，續之則憂；鶴脛雖長，斷之則悲。故性長非所斷，性短非所續，無所去憂也。意仁義其非人情乎！彼仁人何其多憂也？且夫駢於拇者，決之則泣；枝於手者，齕之則啼。二者或有餘於數，或不足於數，其於憂一也。今世之仁人，蒿目而憂世之患；不仁之人，決性命之情而饕富貴。故意仁義其非人情乎！自三代以下者，天下何其囂囂也？
**PY:** pián mǔ zhī zhǐ， chū hū xìng zāi！ ér chǐ yú dé。 fù zhuì xiàn yóu， chū hū xíng zāi！ ér chǐ yú xìng。 duō fāng hū rén yì ér yòng zhī zhě， liè yú wǔ cáng zāi！ ér fēi dào dé zhī zhèng yě。 shì gù pián yú zú zhě， lián wú yòng zhī ròu yě； zhī yú shǒu zhě， shù wú yòng zhī zhǐ yě； duō fāng pián zhī yú wǔ cáng zhī qíng zhě， yín pì yú rén yì zhī xíng， ér duō fāng yú cōng míng zhī yòng yě。 shì gù pián yú míng zhě， luàn wǔ sè， yín wén zhāng， qīng huáng fǔ fú zhī huáng huáng fēi hū？ ér lí zhū shì yǐ。 duō yú cōng zhě， luàn wǔ shēng， yín liù lǜ， jīn shí、 sī zhú， huáng zhōng、 dà lǚ zhī shēng fēi hū？ ér shī kuàng shì yǐ。 zhī yú rén zhě， zhuó dé sāi xìng yǐ shōu míng shēng， shǐ tiān xià huáng gǔ yǐ fèng bù jí zhī fǎ fēi hū？ ér céng、 shǐ shì yǐ。 pián yú biàn zhě， léi wǎ jié shéng cuàn jù， yóu xīn yú jiān bái tóng yì zhī jiān， ér bì kuǐ yù wú yòng zhī yán fēi hū？ ér yáng、 mò shì yǐ。 gù cǐ jiē duō pián páng zhī zhī dào， fēi tiān xià zhī zhì zhèng yě。 bǐ zhèng zhèng zhě， bù shī qí xìng mìng zhī qíng。 gù hé zhě bù wèi pián， ér zhī zhě bù wèi qí； zhǎng zhě bù wèi yǒu yú， duǎn zhě bù wèi bù zú。 shì gù fú jìng suī duǎn， xù zhī zé yōu； hè jìng suī zhǎng， duàn zhī zé bēi。 gù xìng zhǎng fēi suǒ duàn， xìng duǎn fēi suǒ xù， wú suǒ qù yōu yě。 yì rén yì qí fēi rén qíng hū！ bǐ rén rén hé qí duō yōu yě？ qiě fū pián yú mǔ zhě， jué zhī zé qì； zhī yú shǒu zhě， hé zhī zé tí。 èr zhě huò yǒu yú yú shù， huò bù zú yú shù， qí yú yōu yī yě。 jīn shì zhī rén rén， hāo mù ér yōu shì zhī huàn； bù rén zhī rén， jué xìng mìng zhī qíng ér tāo fù guì。 gù yì rén yì qí fēi rén qíng hū！ zì sān dài yǐ xià zhě， tiān xià hé qí xiāo xiāo yě？

**EN:** Webbed toes and branching fingers — they come out of nature, but they exceed what virtue requires. Hanging warts and dangling tumors — they come out of the form, but they exceed what nature requires. The many methods of humaneness and rightness, and those who employ them — they are ranked among the five organs, but they are not the rectitude of the Way and its virtue. Therefore what is webbed on the foot joins useless flesh; what is branched on the hand sets up a useless finger; what is webbed and branched beyond the genuine condition of the five organs runs wild and perverse in the practice of humaneness and rightness, and multiplies methods in the use of hearing and sight. Therefore what is webbed onto sight disorders the five colors, runs wild in patterned ornament — is it not the blazing blue-and-yellow of embroidered robes? Such was Li Zhu. What is multiplied onto hearing disorders the five notes, runs wild in the six pitch-pipes, in the sounds of metal and stone, silk and bamboo, Yellow Bell and Great Tube — is it not so? Such was Music Master Kuang. What is branched onto humaneness plucks up virtue and stops up nature to gather name and fame, making the world pipe and drum to serve an unattainable model — is it not so? Such were Zeng and Shi. What is webbed onto disputation piles up tiles, knots cords, and slips in phrases; it roams the mind among hard-and-white and same-and-different, wearing itself out over the praise of useless sayings — is it not so? Such were Yang and Mo. All these are the way of superfluous webbing and side-branching — not the utmost rectitude of the world. That which is rectifying-rectitude does not lose the genuine condition of its inborn lot. So what is joined is not webbing; what is branched is not a superfluity; the long is not a surplus; the short is not a lack. Therefore a duck's legs are short, but to lengthen them would be a sorrow; a crane's legs are long, but to shorten them would be a grief. What nature makes long is not to be shortened; what nature makes short is not to be lengthened — there is nothing that sorrow need be removed from. Ah — can humaneness and rightness really be the genuine feelings of man! What an abundance of worry those humane men have! Moreover: one whose toes are webbed weeps when they are cut apart; one whose fingers are branched howls when they are bitten off. Of these two, one exceeds the count and one falls short of it — but in worry they are one. The humane men of the present age, with troubled eyes, worry over the calamities of the age; the inhumane men burst through the genuine condition of their inborn lot and glut themselves on riches and rank. Ah — can humaneness and rightness really be the genuine feelings of man! Since the three dynasties, how the world has howled and clamored!

## Section 2

**ZH:** 且夫待鉤繩規矩而正者，是削其性；待繩約膠漆而固者，是侵其德也；屈折禮樂，呴俞仁義，以慰天下之心者，此失其常然也。天下有常然。常然者，曲者不以鉤，直者不以繩，圓者不以規，方者不以矩，附離不以膠漆，約束不以纆索。故天下誘然皆生，而不知其所以生；同焉皆得，而不知其所以得。故古今不二，不可虧也。則仁義又奚連連如膠漆纆索，而遊乎道德之間為哉？使天下惑也！夫小惑易方，大惑易性。何以知其然邪？自虞氏招仁義以撓天下也，天下莫不奔命於仁義，是非以仁義易其性與？
**PY:** qiě fū dài gōu shéng guī jǔ ér zhèng zhě， shì xuē qí xìng； dài shéng yuē jiāo qī ér gù zhě， shì qīn qí dé yě； qū shé lǐ lè， xǔ yú rén yì， yǐ wèi tiān xià zhī xīn zhě， cǐ shī qí cháng rán yě。 tiān xià yǒu cháng rán。 cháng rán zhě， qū zhě bù yǐ gōu， zhí zhě bù yǐ shéng， yuán zhě bù yǐ guī， fāng zhě bù yǐ jǔ， fù lí bù yǐ jiāo qī， yuē shù bù yǐ mò suǒ。 gù tiān xià yòu rán jiē shēng， ér bù zhī qí suǒ yǐ shēng； tóng yān jiē dé， ér bù zhī qí suǒ yǐ dé。 gù gǔ jīn bù èr， bù kě kuī yě。 zé rén yì yòu xī lián lián rú jiāo qī mò suǒ， ér yóu hū dào dé zhī jiān wèi zāi？ shǐ tiān xià huò yě！ fū xiǎo huò yì fāng， dà huò yì xìng。 hé yǐ zhī qí rán xié？ zì yú shì zhāo rén yì yǐ náo tiān xià yě， tiān xià mò bù bèn mìng yú rén yì， shì fēi yǐ rén yì yì qí xìng yǔ？

**EN:** Moreover, what waits on hook, line, compass, and square to be made right is having its nature cut away; what waits on cords, knots, glue, and lacquer to be made firm is having its virtue invaded; to bend and bow with ritual and music, to coo and soothe with humaneness and rightness, so as to comfort the hearts of the world — this is to lose their constancy. The world has a constancy. The constant is: the curved without the hook, the straight without the line, the round without the compass, the square without the square; attachments without glue and lacquer; bindings without cords and ropes. So the world, drawn on, all lives, without knowing how it lives; being one, all attains, without knowing how it attains. Therefore past and present are not two; it cannot be diminished. Then why must humaneness and rightness trail on and on like glue, lacquer, cord, and rope, roaming in the midst of the Way and its virtue? It throws the world into delusion! A small delusion changes one's direction; a great delusion changes one's nature. How do I know it is so? Ever since the house of Yu raised up humaneness and rightness to stir the world, none in the world has not raced its life out after humaneness and rightness. Is this not to exchange one's nature for humaneness and rightness?

## Section 3

**ZH:** 故嘗試論之，自三代以下者，天下莫不以物易其性矣。小人則以身殉利，士則以身殉名，大夫則以身殉家，聖人則以身殉天下。故此數子者，事業不同，名聲異號，其於傷性以身為殉，一也。臧與穀，二人相與牧羊，而俱亡其羊。問臧奚事，則挾筴讀書；問穀奚事，則博塞以遊。二人者，事業不同，其於亡羊均也。伯夷死名於首陽之下，盜跖死利於東陵之上。二人者，所死不同，其於殘生傷性均也，奚必伯夷之是而盜跖之非乎？天下盡殉也。彼其所殉仁義也，則俗謂之君子；其所殉貨財也，則俗謂之小人。其殉一也，則有君子焉，有小人焉；若其殘生損性，則盜跖亦伯夷已，又惡取君子小人於其間哉？且夫屬其性乎仁義者，雖通如曾、史，非吾所謂臧也；屬其性於五味，雖通如俞兒，非吾所謂臧也；屬其性乎五聲，雖通如師曠，非吾所謂聰也；屬其性乎五色，雖通如離朱，非吾所謂明也。吾所謂臧者，非仁義之謂也，臧於其德而已矣；吾所謂臧者，非所謂仁義之謂也，任其性命之情而已矣；吾所謂聰者，非謂其聞彼也，自聞而已矣；吾所謂明者，非謂其見彼也，自見而已矣。夫不自見而見彼，不自得而得彼者，是得人之得而不自得其得者也，適人之適而不自適其適者也。夫適人之適而不自適其適，雖盜跖與伯夷，是同為淫僻也。余愧乎道德，是以上不敢為仁義之操，而下不敢為淫僻之行也。
**PY:** gù cháng shì lùn zhī， zì sān dài yǐ xià zhě， tiān xià mò bù yǐ wù yì qí xìng yǐ。 xiǎo rén zé yǐ shēn xùn lì， shì zé yǐ shēn xùn míng， dà fū zé yǐ shēn xùn jiā， shèng rén zé yǐ shēn xùn tiān xià。 gù cǐ shù zi zhě， shì yè bù tóng， míng shēng yì hào， qí yú shāng xìng yǐ shēn wèi xùn， yī yě。 zāng yǔ gǔ， èr rén xiāng yǔ mù yáng， ér jù wáng qí yáng。 wèn zāng xī shì， zé xié cè dú shū； wèn gǔ xī shì， zé bó sāi yǐ yóu。 èr rén zhě， shì yè bù tóng， qí yú wáng yáng jūn yě。 bó yí sǐ míng yú shǒu yáng zhī xià， dào zhí sǐ lì yú dōng líng zhī shàng。 èr rén zhě， suǒ sǐ bù tóng， qí yú cán shēng shāng xìng jūn yě， xī bì bó yí zhī shì ér dào zhí zhī fēi hū？ tiān xià jǐn xùn yě。 bǐ qí suǒ xùn rén yì yě， zé sú wèi zhī jūn zǐ； qí suǒ xùn huò cái yě， zé sú wèi zhī xiǎo rén。 qí xùn yī yě， zé yǒu jūn zǐ yān， yǒu xiǎo rén yān； ruò qí cán shēng sǔn xìng， zé dào zhí yì bó yí yǐ， yòu è qǔ jūn zǐ xiǎo rén yú qí jiān zāi？ qiě fū shǔ qí xìng hū rén yì zhě， suī tōng rú céng、 shǐ， fēi wú suǒ wèi zāng yě； shǔ qí xìng yú wǔ wèi， suī tōng rú yú ér， fēi wú suǒ wèi zāng yě； shǔ qí xìng hū wǔ shēng， suī tōng rú shī kuàng， fēi wú suǒ wèi cōng yě； shǔ qí xìng hū wǔ sè， suī tōng rú lí zhū， fēi wú suǒ wèi míng yě。 wú suǒ wèi zāng zhě， fēi rén yì zhī wèi yě， zāng yú qí dé ér yǐ yǐ； wú suǒ wèi zāng zhě， fēi suǒ wèi rén yì zhī wèi yě， rèn qí xìng mìng zhī qíng ér yǐ yǐ； wú suǒ wèi cōng zhě， fēi wèi qí wén bǐ yě， zì wén ér yǐ yǐ； wú suǒ wèi míng zhě， fēi wèi qí jiàn bǐ yě， zì jiàn ér yǐ yǐ。 fū bù zì jiàn ér jiàn bǐ， bù zì dé ér dé bǐ zhě， shì dé rén zhī dé ér bù zì dé qí dé zhě yě， shì rén zhī shì ér bù zì shì qí shì zhě yě。 fū shì rén zhī shì ér bù zì shì qí shì， suī dào zhí yǔ bó yí， shì tóng wèi yín pì yě。 yú kuì hū dào dé， shì yǐ shàng bù gǎn wèi rén yì zhī cāo， ér xià bù gǎn wèi yín pì zhī xíng yě。

**EN:** So let me try to argue it: since the three dynasties, there is no one in the world who has not exchanged his nature for things. The small man martyrs his person to profit; the scholar martyrs his person to fame; the great officer martyrs his person to his family; the sage martyrs his person to the world. These several men — their undertakings differ; the names by which they are famed differ; but in injuring their nature and martyring their persons, they are one. Zang and Gu, two men, were shepherding sheep together, and both lost their sheep. Ask Zang what he was doing — he was holding his tablets, reading. Ask Gu what he was doing — he was playing pitch-and-toss. The two men's undertakings differ; in losing the sheep they are equal. Boyi died for a name at the foot of Shouyang; Robber Zhi died for profit atop Eastern Mound. The two men died for different things; in maiming life and injuring nature they are equal. Why must Boyi be right and Robber Zhi wrong? All the world is martyred. What one martyrs himself to being humaneness and rightness, custom calls a gentleman; what he martyrs himself to being goods and wealth, custom calls a small man. The martyrdom is one — yet there are gentlemen and small men. As for maiming life and injuring nature, Robber Zhi is also Boyi — where is the use in taking gentleman and small man from between them? Moreover: one who binds his nature to humaneness and rightness — though he be as penetrating as Zeng or Shi — is not what I call good; one who binds his nature to the five flavors — though he be as penetrating as Yu'er (俞兒) — is not what I call good; one who binds his nature to the five notes — though he be as penetrating as Music Master Kuang — is not what I call hearing; one who binds his nature to the five colors — though he be as penetrating as Li Zhu — is not what I call seeing. What I call good is not humaneness and rightness; it is goodness in one's virtue, and that is all. What I call good is not the so-called humaneness and rightness; it is letting be the genuine condition of one's inborn lot, and that is all. What I call hearing is not hearing that other; it is hearing oneself, and that is all. What I call seeing is not seeing that other; it is seeing oneself, and that is all. To fail to see oneself and see that other, to fail to attain oneself and attain that other — this is to attain others' attainments without attaining one's own attainment; to suit others' suiting without suiting one's own suiting. To suit others' suiting without suiting one's own suiting — be it Robber Zhi or Boyi — is alike to be wild and perverse. I am ashamed before the Way and its virtue; therefore above I dare not practice the conduct of humaneness and rightness, and below I dare not practice the conduct of wildness and perversity.

## Translator's Note

- Glossary consistency: 性 → "nature"; 性命之情 → "the genuine condition of the inborn lot"; 德 → "virtue"; 道德 → "the Way and its virtue"; 聰/明 → "hearing"/"seeing" (used reflexively at the close: 自聞 "hearing oneself," 自見 "seeing oneself" — echoing 大宗師's "not suiting one's own suiting" formula).
- The chapter is the Outer Chapters' opening salvo against the Confucian virtues as excrescences; its rhetoric of "webbing" and "branching" is kept literal.
