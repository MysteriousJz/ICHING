# Horses' Hoofs (馬蹄)
Source: Zhuangzi _ Outer Chapters _ Horses's Hoofs - Chinese Text Project.html
Status: complete (3 sections)

## Section 1

**ZH:** 馬，蹄可以踐霜雪，毛可以禦風寒，齕草飲水，翹足而陸。此馬之真性也。雖有義臺、路寢，無所用之。及至伯樂，曰：「我善治馬。」燒之剔之，刻之雒之，連之以羈馽，編之以皁棧，馬之死者十二三矣；飢之渴之，馳之驟之，整之齊之，前有橛飾之患，而後有鞭筴之威，而馬之死者已過半矣。陶者曰：「我善治埴，圓者中規，方者中矩。」匠人曰：「我善治木，曲者中鉤，直者應繩。」夫埴、木之性，豈欲中規矩鉤繩哉？然且世世稱之曰：「伯樂善治馬，而陶、匠善治埴木。」此亦治天下者之過也。
**PY:** mǎ， tí kě yǐ jiàn shuāng xuě， máo kě yǐ yù fēng hán， hé cǎo yǐn shuǐ， qiào zú ér lù。 cǐ mǎ zhī zhēn xìng yě。 suī yǒu yì tái、 lù qǐn， wú suǒ yòng zhī。 jí zhì bó lè， yuē：「 wǒ shàn zhì mǎ。」 shāo zhī tī zhī， kè zhī luò zhī， lián zhī yǐ jī zhí， biān zhī yǐ zào zhàn， mǎ zhī sǐ zhě shí èr sān yǐ； jī zhī kě zhī， chí zhī zhòu zhī， zhěng zhī qí zhī， qián yǒu jué shì zhī huàn， ér hòu yǒu biān cè zhī wēi， ér mǎ zhī sǐ zhě yǐ guò bàn yǐ。 táo zhě yuē：「 wǒ shàn zhì zhí， yuán zhě zhōng guī， fāng zhě zhōng jǔ。」 jiàng rén yuē：「 wǒ shàn zhì mù， qū zhě zhōng gōu， zhí zhě yīng shéng。」 fū zhí、 mù zhī xìng， qǐ yù zhōng guī jǔ gōu shéng zāi？ rán qiě shì shì chēng zhī yuē：「 bó lè shàn zhì mǎ， ér táo、 jiàng shàn zhì zhí mù。」 cǐ yì zhì tiān xià zhě zhī guò yě。

**EN:** Horses: their hoofs can tread frost and snow, their coats can withstand wind and cold. They crop the grass and drink the water; they paw the ground and leap. This is the true nature of horses. Even given grand terraces and lofty halls, they would have no use for them. Then along came Bole (伯樂), saying: "I am good at handling horses." He singed them, clipped them, shaved them, and branded them; he tied them with halter and hobble, penned them in stall and stable — and two or three out of ten horses died. He starved them, parched them, galloped them, raced them, lined them up and ranked them — before them the misery of bit and ornament, behind them the terror of whip and crop — and more than half the horses died. The potter says: "I am good at handling clay: round pieces fit the compass, square pieces fit the square." The carpenter says: "I am good at handling wood: curved pieces fit the hook, straight pieces answer the line." But is it the nature of clay and wood to wish to fit compass, square, hook, and line? Yet generation after generation praises them, saying: "Bole is good at handling horses; the potter and carpenter are good at handling clay and wood." This is the same fault as that of those who govern the world.

## Section 2

**ZH:** 吾意善治天下者不然。彼民有常性，織而衣，耕而食，是謂同德；一而不黨，命曰天放。故至德之世，其行填填，其視顛顛。當是時也，山無蹊隧，澤無舟梁；萬物群生，連屬其鄉；禽獸成群，草木遂長。是故禽獸可係羈而遊，烏鵲之巢可攀援而闚。夫至德之世，同與禽獸居，族與萬物並，惡乎知君子小人哉！同乎無知，其德不離；同乎無欲，是謂素樸。素樸而民性得矣。及至聖人，蹩躠為仁，踶跂為義，而天下始疑矣；澶漫為樂，摘僻為禮，而天下始分矣。故純樸不殘，孰為犧尊！白玉不毀，孰為珪璋！道德不廢，安取仁義！性情不離，安用禮樂！五色不亂，孰為文采！五聲不亂，孰應六律！夫殘樸以為器，工匠之罪也；毀道德以為仁義，聖人之過也。
**PY:** wú yì shàn zhì tiān xià zhě bù rán。 bǐ mín yǒu cháng xìng， zhī ér yī， gēng ér shí， shì wèi tóng dé； yī ér bù dǎng， mìng yuē tiān fàng。 gù zhì dé zhī shì， qí xíng tián tián， qí shì diān diān。 dāng shì shí yě， shān wú qī suì， zé wú zhōu liáng； wàn wù qún shēng， lián shǔ qí xiāng； qín shòu chéng qún， cǎo mù suì zhǎng。 shì gù qín shòu kě xì jī ér yóu， wū què zhī cháo kě pān yuán ér kuī。 fū zhì dé zhī shì， tóng yǔ qín shòu jū， zú yǔ wàn wù bìng， è hū zhī jūn zǐ xiǎo rén zāi！ tóng hū wú zhī， qí dé bù lí； tóng hū wú yù， shì wèi sù pǔ。 sù pǔ ér mín xìng dé yǐ。 jí zhì shèng rén， bié sǎ wèi rén， dì qí wèi yì， ér tiān xià shǐ yí yǐ； chán màn wèi lè， zhāi pì wèi lǐ， ér tiān xià shǐ fēn yǐ。 gù chún pǔ bù cán， shú wèi xī zūn！ bái yù bù huǐ， shú wèi guī zhāng！ dào dé bù fèi， ān qǔ rén yì！ xìng qíng bù lí， ān yòng lǐ lè！ wǔ sè bù luàn， shú wèi wén cǎi！ wǔ shēng bù luàn， shú yīng liù lǜ！ fū cán pǔ yǐ wèi qì， gōng jiàng zhī zuì yě； huǐ dào dé yǐ wèi rén yì， shèng rén zhī guò yě。

**EN:** I maintain that one who was truly good at governing the world would not be like this. The people have a constant nature: they weave and are clothed, they plough and are fed. This is called shared virtue. United and without factions, it is named Heaven's setting-free. Thus in the age of utmost virtue, the people walked slow and sure, and looked straight ahead. In those times there were no paths or tunnels through the mountains, no boats or bridges over the marshes. The ten thousand things lived in droves, their settlements adjoining one another; birds and beasts formed their herds, grass and trees grew unchecked. So birds and beasts could be led about on a rope, and one could climb up and peer into the magpie's nest. In the age of utmost virtue, men dwelt together with the birds and beasts, their kind side by side with the ten thousand things — how could they have known of gentleman and small man! Being one in having no knowledge, their virtue never left them; being one in having no desires — this is called the plain and uncarved. Plain and uncarved, the people's nature was attained. Then along came the sages, tottering and straining after humaneness, rearing up on tiptoe after rightness — and for the first time the world fell into doubt. Overflowing in music, finicky in ritual — and for the first time the world was split apart. If the uncarved block were not mutilated, who would make a sacrificial goblet! If the white jade were not shattered, who would make a scepter or tablet! If the Way and its virtue were not abandoned, where would humaneness and rightness be got from! If nature and genuine feeling were not forsaken, what use would ritual and music be! If the five colors were not confused, who would make the patterned ornament! If the five notes were not confused, who would answer to the six pitch-pipes! To mutilate the uncarved block to make vessels of it — that is the crime of the artisan. To destroy the Way and its virtue to make humaneness and rightness — that is the fault of the sage.

## Section 3

**ZH:** 夫馬，陸居則食草飲水，喜則交頸相靡，怒則分背相踶。馬知已此矣。夫加之以衡扼，齊之以月題，而馬知介倪、闉扼、鷙曼、詭銜、竊轡。故馬之知而態至盜者，伯樂之罪也。夫赫胥氏之時，民居不知所為，行不知所之，含哺而熙，鼓腹而遊，民能以此矣。及至聖人，屈折禮樂以匡天下之形，縣跂仁義以慰天下之心，而民乃始踶跂好知，爭歸於利，不可止也。此亦聖人之過也。
**PY:** fū mǎ， lù jū zé shí cǎo yǐn shuǐ， xǐ zé jiāo jǐng xiāng mí， nù zé fēn bèi xiāng dì。 mǎ zhī yǐ cǐ yǐ。 fū jiā zhī yǐ héng è， qí zhī yǐ yuè tí， ér mǎ zhī jiè ní、 yīn è、 zhì màn、 guǐ xián、 qiè pèi。 gù mǎ zhī zhī ér tài zhì dào zhě， bó lè zhī zuì yě。 fū hè xū shì zhī shí， mín jū bù zhī suǒ wèi， xíng bù zhī suǒ zhī， hán bǔ ér xī， gǔ fù ér yóu， mín néng yǐ cǐ yǐ。 jí zhì shèng rén， qū shé lǐ lè yǐ kuāng tiān xià zhī xíng， xiàn qí rén yì yǐ wèi tiān xià zhī xīn， ér mín nǎi shǐ dì qí hǎo zhī， zhēng guī yú lì， bù kě zhǐ yě。 cǐ yì shèng rén zhī guò yě。

**EN:** Horses live on the land, eat grass and drink water; when pleased, they twine necks and rub one another; when angry, they turn rump to rump and kick. This is the limit of horses' knowledge. But put the yoke across them, line them up with the moon-frontlet on their brows, and horses learn to slant their eyes to bite, to turn back and butt the yoke, to fight the mane-cover, to spit the bit and steal loose of the reins. [uncertain: 介倪、闉扼、鷙曼、詭銜、竊轡 — a list of five evasive tricks of harnessed horses; the exact mechanics of each term are variously glossed.] That the knowledge of horses comes to the very semblance of robbery — that is the crime of Bole. In the time of Hexu (赫胥氏), the people at rest did not know what they were doing; on the move, they did not know where they were going. Their mouths full of food, they played; drumming on their bellies, they roamed. This was the limit of the people's ability. Then along came the sages, bending and bowing with ritual and music to correct the form of the world, dangling humaneness and rightness on high to comfort the world's heart — and the people for the first time reared up on tiptoe in their love of knowledge, scrambling after profit, beyond all stopping. This too is the fault of the sage.
